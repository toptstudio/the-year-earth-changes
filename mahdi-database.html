The Shia Tradition

The term Shia (Shi'atu Ali) means the "followers of Ali." In Twelver Shi'i doctrine, leadership of the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad (SAW) was divinely designated to Ali ibn Abi Talib (A.S.) and eleven of his descendants, collectively known as the Twelve Imams. Within that tradition, this is presented not as a political preference but as a theological requirement grounded in the Quran and Sunnah. The Imams are infallible (ma'sum), divinely chosen guides, and the authoritative interpreters of Islam within the Twelver doctrinal framework. Their authority is called Wilayah, the spiritual and temporal guardianship that completes faith.

The Twelver Shi'i position holds that the Prophet explicitly announced this succession at Ghadeer Khumm, in his final year, by declaring: "For whomever I am his master (mawla), Ali is his master." This was not mere friendship but the bestowal of spiritual authority. The Imamate continues through twelve members of the Ahl al-Bayt, each appointed by the preceding Imam, culminating in the living yet hidden Imam Muhammad al-Mahdi (A.S.), who will reappear to fill the earth with justice. Within the Twelver reading, these texts form a connected case; individual reports and their interpretation remain matters for source-by-source examination.

The Rajʿah
The Return Before the Hour

Rajʿah is a pre-Resurrection return to this world, strictly limited to the purest believers and the most wicked disbelievers. It is distinct from Qiyamah, serving as a unique divine event for justice and retribution. The following verses and narrations clarify its place in the end-times timeline.

قَالَ: ذَلِكَ فِي الرَّجْعَةِ

"That is concerning the Rajʿah or return."

— Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 53, p. 116, hadith 157.

After the Day of Judgment, neither believers nor disbelievers will ever face death again.
They will remain in their final states eternally.

However, scriptural verses explicitly mention "two deaths," whereas a standard earthly life only experiences a single physical death. This contradiction proves that the second death must take place after the Rajʿah, applying specifically to the distinct groups of believers and disbelievers who are returned to Earth during that period.

Moreover, in this verse, "twice death" is mentioned first,
and only after it does it talk about "twice life".

Why two lives and not three?

Every person already lives a first life before death, so after the first death the Rajʿah or return would be a second life, and after the second death the final resurrection would be a third life. That would give three lives, not two.

Answer: The verse does not count the first worldly life as "giving life" (iḥyāʾ).
The words "You gave us life twice" refer to reviving after death, not to the original creation.
The first life from birth is creation,
not a revival from a previous death.
So the two lives in the verse are:

1. The life of the Rajʿah or return, after the first death.
2. The life of the final resurrection,
after the second death.

Thus the timeline is:

First life - Creation
First death

Second life - Rajʿah or return
Second death

Third existence - Final resurrection
But only two revivals from death,
matching the verse.

First death: The first death is the normal death at the end of this worldly life. It occurs for all mankind. Including the special groups who will later be returned and then die again a second death.

So:

  • ●
    First death — for all people, including for those destined for Rajʿah or return.
  • ●
    Second death — only for the two returned groups of Rajʿah or return, including both teams/groups: the pure believers, the pure disbelievers.

The purpose of Rajʿah is revenge and retribution:

As established in Bihar al-Anwar, the Rajʿah or return is far more than a simple return to life; it is a divinely decreed day of retribution and cosmic justice. During this period, oppressed believers will return to Earth to exact vengeance upon the wrongdoers who persecuted them, while the disbelievers and oppressors will face humiliation and punishment in this world prior to their final torment in the Hereafter. Consequently, prophetic traditions link the Rajʿah directly to Yawm al-Qa’im (the Day of the Qa’im) and Yawm al-Karrah (the Day of the Return) —the monumental epoch where absolute justice is executed, the faithful emerge victorious, and the enemies of Allah and the Ahl al-Bayt are decisively punished.

لَيْسَ هُوَ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ، إِنَّمَا هُوَ الرَّجْعَةُ

"It is not the Day of Resurrection, but rather the Rajʿah."

— Imam al-Sadiq, Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 53, p. 39, hadith 1.

وَإِنَّمَا يُحْشَرُ فِي الرَّجْعَةِ قَوْمٌ مَحَضُوا الْإِيمَانَ مَحْضًا وَقَوْمٌ مَحَضُوا الْكُفْرَ مَحْضًا

"Only those who were purely faithful and those who were purely unbelieving will be resurrected in the Rajʿah or return."

— Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 53, p. 39, hadith 1.

This confirms that Raj'ah (the return) is limited to only two specific groups the exceptionally righteous believers and the most obstinate, staunch disbelievers.

أَيَّامُ اللَّهِ ثَلَاثَةٌ: يَوْمُ الْقَائِمِ، وَيَوْمُ الْكَرَّةِ، وَيَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ

"The Days of Allah are three:
the Day of the Qa'im,
the Day of the Return, and the Day of Resurrection."

— Imam al-Baqir, Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 53, p. 63.

The "Days of Allah" include the Rajʿah or return, indicating it is a separate epoch before the final judgement.

أَوَّلُ مَنْ يَرْجِعُ إِلَى الدُّنْيَا الْحُسَيْنُ بْنُ عَلِيٍّ عَلَيْهِمَا السَّلَامُ

"The first one to return to this world will be al-Husayn ibn Ali."

— Imam al-Sadiq, Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 53, p. 46.

The return begins with Imam al-Husayn, a symbol of justice for the oppressed.

The Quran already shows instances of divine revival before the final Day, demonstrating that the Rajʿah or return is consistent with Allah's power and precedent.

These verses confirm that death ceases to exist after the final judgment. Therefore, the second death mentioned in Qur'an 40:11 must take place prior to Qiyamah, occurring specifically after the Rajʿah or return.

Quran 40:11 explicitly outlines a timeline of two deaths and two lives, yet a standard earthly existence yields only a single physical death. Furthermore, death is entirely eliminated after the Day of Judgment, as believers abide eternally in Paradise (Quran 44:56) and disbelievers remain forever in Hell (Quran 35:36).

Consequently, the second death must occur prior to Qiyamah, taking place specifically at the conclusion of the Rajʿah. This return is reserved exclusively for two distinct groups—pure believers and pure disbelievers—and serves the divine purpose of justice and retribution. Through it, the resurrected believers exact vengeance upon those who wronged them, ensuring the oppressors suffer humiliation and punishment in this world before facing their final torment in the Hereafter.

Then those returned groups die the second death and after that comes the final resurrection and eternal life without any death.

Frequently Asked Questions

"Why does the Mahdi's name not appear in Bukhari or Muslim?"

Because the Abbasids, who controlled the compilation of hadith, were enemies of the Ahl al‑Bayt. They suppressed what they could not destroy. The Mahdi appears in many other canonical collections, proving the suppression was not total.

"Is it not a Shia belief to consider the Imams infallible?"

Infallibility is a logical necessity of the hadith that says "whoever obeys them obeys Allah." If any of them sinned, obedience would become disobedience. The Shia doctrine of infallibility is the only one that meets this requirement.

"What about the hadith that says the caliphate will last 30 years?"

That hadith refers to the Rashidun caliphate as a model of governance, not the complete list of twelve. The twelve are a separate, distinct prophecy that spans the entire history of Islam until the Hour.

"Why do many Sunni scholars reject the Shia list?"

Most reject it for political reasons, not scholarly ones. To accept the Shia list would invalidate the legitimacy of the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates. The rejection is motivated by power, not evidence.

"What is the Shia meaning of Wilayah?"

Wilayah is the spiritual guardianship and authority that Allah vested in the Prophet and his twelve successors. It includes both political leadership and the esoteric interpretation of the Quran. Accepting the Wilayah of the Imams is part of the completion of faith, as per the verse of Ghadeer.

"How can the 12th Imam be alive for over a millennium?"

The Quran provides precedents: Prophet Nuh lived 950 years, and Khidr is believed to be alive. Moreover, those who mock the Mahdi's long life readily accept that Iblis, Dajjal, and Jesus have survived for thousands of years. Allah is capable of prolonging the life of His proof on earth. The Mahdi's occultation is a miracle and a divine test.

"What is the difference between Imam and Caliph?"

A Caliph in Sunni understanding is a political successor elected by people or through conquest. An Imam in Shia doctrine is a divinely appointed, infallible leader, endowed with comprehensive knowledge and spiritual authority. The twelve successors in the hadith are Imams, not mere caliphs, because their obedience is equated with obedience to Allah without condition.

"Do Shia have a different Quran?"

No. The Shia use the same Quran as all Muslims. The difference lies in interpretation, which the Imams provided. The Quran commands its followers to seek knowledge from the "People of Remembrance" (Ahl al-Dhikr), who are the Imams.

"What does the 'Alif' symbolize in Shah Ni‘matullah's prophecies?"

The site reads the “Alif” in Shah Ni‘matullah’s poetry as a symbolic reference to a dominant modern power. Because that identification depends on later interpretation rather than an explicit name in the text, it should be treated as conjectural rather than as a literal prediction.
